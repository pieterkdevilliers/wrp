**Why You're Working Harder Than Ever**

These are some questions I get asked by business owners, not in boardrooms or strategy sessions, but in real conversations with people who are tired, stretched, and trying to figure out where it's all going wrong. This week I want to work through five of them, because the answers tend to connect in ways that aren't always obvious at first.

### Why Are You Working Longer Hours Than When You Started?

This one is almost universal. In the early days, you're working hard to get the business off the ground. That's expected. The problem is when nothing changes after lift-off. More clients, more work, more demands, and if you haven't handed anything off to people or to systems, the only possible outcome is that you're doing more. So you end up doing quotes at 11 o'clock at night, chasing invoices over the weekend, and wondering where your evenings went.

The question to ask isn't how many hours you're working. It's what you're actually doing with those hours. If you're pushing the business forward, supporting growth, working on things only you can do, it is still hard, but you know you are doing the right things to build a valuable business. If you're doing work that a system or a team member could handle, that's a different problem entirely. One is the aeroplane straining to get airborne. The other is the aeroplane sitting on the runway with the engines running, going nowhere.

### Is Being the Hardest Worker in Your Business a Strength or a Weakness?

Both, depending on what you're working hard at.

Most people who start businesses are wired to outwork everyone around them. That instinct is what got them here. But if you're the first on site swinging a hammer and the last to leave, and then you go home and do all the admin on top of that, you're not building a business, you're building a job with unlimited hours and no overtime.

The trap is the thinking: it's quicker if I just do it myself. That might be true in the moment. But if you apply that logic to everything, you end up on a hamster wheel. Working flat out, but not actually moving forward. You can work incredibly hard at the wrong things and still stay stuck. The work ethic isn't the problem. What you are investing your effort into might be.

### How Do You Create Accountability Without Micromanaging?

The core principle here is that you have to allow your team to make mistakes. If you're checking every step as they do it, correcting them in real time, standing over their shoulder, they don't learn and they don't trust you. And you don't get to build a business worth anything.

A better model is to think about guardrails rather than control. Like the bumpers in a children's bowling lane, they don't play the game for the child, they just stop the ball going in the gutter every time. The child still needs to improve their aim, still needs coaching, but the guardrails give them a framework to operate within. Your systems and processes do the same thing for your team.

Then establish a rhythm. Every Monday morning: what went well last week, what needs improving, what are we doing this week and how do we apply what we learned? It's not a performance review, it's a debrief, like a sports team reviewing Saturday's match on Monday so they play better next weekend. Done right, the team knows they're accountable, they know mistakes will be examined but not weaponised, and they know you actually trust them to do the work. That's real accountability.

### What AI Tools Are Actually Useful for a Service Business Right Now?

I'm reluctant to hand you a list. The tools change constantly, and the bigger risk is reshaping your business around a tool that someone else designed for a different problem.

What tends to work far better is building small, specific tools for your particular business, things that understand your context, your clients, your workflow. And right now, that's cheaper and more accessible than most people realise. You're not paying for 25 features when you only need three. You get exactly what you need and nothing you don't.

A practical example: we build a review request automation where a client fills in five questions after a job is done, their answers go to an AI prompt, and it generates a suggested review in their words. The customer gets an email saying: "I took the liberty of drafting this from your answers, feel free to edit it or share it as is." That's a small, bespoke tool solving a specific problem. No expensive platform. No learning curve. Just a thing that works, and does work for you.

### What Kind of Business Owner Do You Do Your Best Work With?

The ones who want to iterate, not the ones who want a big reveal.

Think about rewiring a house. You wouldn't run all the cables, plaster the walls, lay the carpets, bring the furniture in, and then on the morning the owner moves in, decide to test if the electrics work. If something's wrong at that point, you've got a nightmare on your hands. The right approach is to test as you go. Run the cables, check for damage, add the next element, test again, keep going.

Our work follows the same logic. We make changes every two weeks, test, adjust, and keep moving. Over a year, that's twenty-five opportunities to correct direction. If you work in big quarterly launches instead, you've got four. Four chances to catch a problem that's been compounding for three months. That's slow, expensive, and frustrating for everyone involved.

The business owners we do our best work with treat this as a partnership. They're not waiting for a finished product to drop into their lap. They're adding, testing, improving, consistently. That's how systems actually get built. That's how businesses actually change.

### The Common Thread

Looking at all five questions together, there's one thing that runs through every answer: the way you spend your time, the way your team operates, and the tools you use all need to work together, and none of it happens by accident. It has to be designed. The businesses that get this right don't necessarily have more resources or smarter people. They've just been more deliberate about where effort goes and what systems support that effort.
